How this works, and what it cannot do

The uncomfortable parts first.

What you should expect

A good outcome is a few percent return, with long losing runs on the way. Independently verified value services sit around 4–7% ROI. Anything advertising 20%+ sustained on daily racing volume is outside everything that has ever been verified.

At a genuine 4% edge and average odds of 5.0, flat stakes:

  • After 1,000 bets you are still losing money about 25% of the time.
  • The median worst drawdown over 5,000 bets is around 107 units.
  • You will hit a losing run of 24 to 31 bets somewhere along the way.
  • A bankroll of 200–300 units is needed to survive that comfortably.

Proving the edge from profit alone would take roughly 7,000 to 23,000 bets — four to seven years at five bets a day. That is why closing line value, not profit, is the headline metric on the performance page.

The model

Two stages, following Bill Benter's 1994 design — the one that made a Hong Kong syndicate close to a billion dollars.

  1. A fundamental model scores every runner from its form history: opposition-adjusted ability ratings, going and distance suitability estimated from the horse's own record, trainer and jockey strike rates shrunk toward the population mean, days since last run, class and field shape. Every feature is computed strictly from races run before the one being priced.
  2. A blend with the market. The model's probabilities are combined with the market's implied probabilities by maximum likelihood. This is the step that matters. Benter called incorporating public odds his single most important innovation: a standalone model is systematically overconfident in its own opinions, and only the blend produces probabilities worth staking on.

The test of the model is therefore not accuracy but added information — how much the blend improves on the market alone. If the model knows nothing the market does not, the blend weight on it collapses to zero and the suggestions stop. That is the intended behaviour, not a failure.

The value test

A suggestion is published only when the best available price clears all three filters:

  • Minimum edge — expected value per unit staked, after exchange commission.
  • Minimum probability — no longshots. Relative probability errors are largest there, and the favourite-longshot bias means bookmaker prices on outsiders are the worst value in racing (backing every 100/1+ shot loses about 61%).
  • Maximum odds — a hard ceiling, for the same reason.

The price floor

Every suggestion carries a floor: the shortest price at which the bet still clears the edge threshold. This is not a formality. Advised prices collapse within minutes of publication — one widely followed tipster's advised average of 10.41 was 7.35 by the time followers actually got on, and independent tracking has measured ROI losses of 4.5 to 24.5 percentage points from taking the price fifteen minutes late. Below the floor there is no bet.

Staking

Quarter-Kelly, capped per bet and per day. Full Kelly is a trap: if you overestimate your advantage by a factor of two, full Kelly produces negative growth. Half-Kelly keeps about three quarters of the growth at half the volatility; a quarter is more forgiving still of a model that is wrong about how right it is.

Where you can actually bet

Bookmakers restrict winning accounts quickly — 643,779 British accounts had restrictions applied in a single year, and surveys of engaged punters put the figure far higher among those who win. Roughly one in five affected punters was cut after fewer than ten bets. A value strategy that works will get its bookmaker accounts limited.

The durable venue is the exchange, where winners are tolerated and charged commission instead, and the Tote pools, which cannot restrict anyone. Bookmaker prices here are shown for comparison and for Best Odds Guaranteed value; plan on the exchange for anything sustained.

What this is not

Furlong takes no bets, holds no money, and places nothing on your behalf. It is an analytics tool that publishes opinions about prices. It cannot tell you which horse will win — nothing can. It estimates probabilities, and looks for prices that are too big for them.
